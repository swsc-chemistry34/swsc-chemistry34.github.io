Stoichiometry and limiting reactants

Part 1 of 5

Moles and the mole ratio

Stoichiometry. Using a balanced equation to work out how much of each substance is used or made.

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the mole ratio. In 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l), 2 mol of hydrogen reacts with 1 mol of oxygen to make 2 mol of water.

To use the ratio, change the amount you are given into moles first.

n = mM
n is the amount in mol. m is the mass in g. M is the molar mass in g mol-1.

The balanced equation and the molar masses always come first. Without them the ratio is wrong.

Part 2 of 5

Mass to mass calculations

Use four steps every time.

  1. Write the balanced equation.
  2. Change the mass you are given into moles.
  3. Use the mole ratio to find the moles of the substance you want.
  4. Change those moles into mass.
Worked example

What mass of carbon dioxide forms when 8.0 g of methane burns completely?

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)
n(CH4) = 8.016.0 = 0.50 mol
M(CH4) = 12.0 + (4 × 1.0) = 16.0 g mol-1.
n(CO2) = 0.50 mol
The ratio of CH4 to CO2 is 1 to 1.
m(CO2) = 0.50 × 44.0 = 22 g
M(CO2) = 12.0 + (2 × 16.0) = 44.0 g mol-1.
Part 3 of 5

Gas volumes at SLC

Molar volume. At standard laboratory conditions (SLC, 25 °C and 100 kPa), 1 mol of any gas has a volume of 24.8 L.
n = V24.8
V is the gas volume in L at SLC. 24.8 is the molar volume in L mol-1.
Worked example

What volume of carbon dioxide forms at SLC when 8.0 g of methane burns completely?

n(CO2) = 0.50 mol   (from Part 2)
V(CO2) = 0.50 × 24.8 = 12.4 L

Gases at the same temperature and pressure react in the same ratio as their moles. So 5.0 L of methane needs 10.0 L of oxygen, and no mole conversion is needed.

Part 4 of 5

Limiting reactants

Limiting reactant. The reactant that runs out first. It decides how much product forms. The other reactant is in excess, so some is left over.

To find the limiting reactant, change both amounts into moles. Divide each by its coefficient. The smaller answer belongs to the limiting reactant.

Worked example

4.0 g of methane burns in 8.0 g of oxygen. Find the limiting reactant, the mass of carbon dioxide, and the methane left over.

n(CH4) = 4.016.0 = 0.25 mol     n(O2) = 8.032.0 = 0.25 mol
Methane: 0.251 = 0.25     Oxygen: 0.252 = 0.125
Oxygen gives the smaller answer, so oxygen is the limiting reactant.
n(CO2) = 0.252 = 0.125 mol     m(CO2) = 0.125 × 44.0 = 5.5 g
The ratio of O2 to CO2 is 2 to 1, so the moles of oxygen are halved.
Methane used = 0.125 mol   Methane left = 0.25 − 0.125 = 0.125 mol = 2.0 g
Part 5 of 5

Why this matters for fuels

When a fuel burns with too little oxygen, oxygen is the limiting reactant. Some fuel is left over, or it burns incompletely to make carbon monoxide and soot.

Industry often adds one cheap reactant in excess on purpose. That makes sure the expensive reactant is used up completely.

Next, the amount of fuel that burns decides how much energy is released. In Period 6 you will multiply the moles of fuel by the enthalpy change.

Check yourself

In the worked example in Part 4, how much more oxygen would 4.0 g of methane need to burn completely?

Show the answer

4.0 g of methane is 0.25 mol. The ratio of CH4 to O2 is 1 to 2, so it needs 0.50 mol of oxygen. It had 0.25 mol, so it needs 0.25 mol more. That is 0.25 × 32.0 = 8.0 g.

Finished the Core work? Try Extend

2.0 g of hydrogen reacts with 8.0 g of oxygen. Write the equation, find the limiting reactant, and find the mass of water formed.

Show the worked solution

2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l)

n(H2) = 2.02.0 = 1.0 mol.   n(O2) = 8.032.0 = 0.25 mol.

Hydrogen: 1.02 = 0.50.   Oxygen: 0.251 = 0.25. Oxygen is smaller, so oxygen is limiting.

n(H2O) = 2 × 0.25 = 0.50 mol.   m(H2O) = 0.50 × 18.0 = 9.0 g.

Exit ticket

Back to the burgers. Each burger needs 2 slices of cheese, so 16 slices make only 8 burgers. Cheese is the limiting ingredient. Buns and patties are in excess. 4 buns and 2 patties are left over.

Write it out

3.0 g of hydrogen reacts with 16.0 g of oxygen to make water. Find the limiting reactant and the mass of water formed.

Show the worked solution

2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l)

n(H2) = 3.02.0 = 1.5 mol.   n(O2) = 16.032.0 = 0.50 mol.

Hydrogen: 1.52 = 0.75.   Oxygen: 0.501 = 0.50. Oxygen is smaller, so oxygen is limiting.

n(H2O) = 2 × 0.50 = 1.0 mol.   m(H2O) = 1.0 × 18.0 = 18 g.

Key words

Stoichiometry
Using a balanced equation to work out how much of each substance is used or made.
Mole ratio
The ratio of the coefficients in a balanced equation.
Molar mass, M
The mass of 1 mol of a substance, in g mol⁻¹.
Amount, n
The number of moles of a substance, in mol.
Molar volume
The volume of 1 mol of any gas. At SLC it is 24.8 L mol⁻¹.
SLC
Standard laboratory conditions: 25 °C and 100 kPa.
Limiting reactant
The reactant that runs out first. It decides how much product forms.
Excess reactant
A reactant that is left over when the limiting reactant has run out.
Watch out

Compare moles, not masses. 8.0 g of oxygen is not the same amount as 8.0 g of methane.

Watch out

Divide by the coefficient before you compare. 0.25 mol of O₂ can only react with 0.125 mol of CH₄.

Watch out

The limiting reactant is not always the one with fewer moles. It is the one with the smaller amount after dividing by its coefficient.

Watch out

Use the limiting reactant, never the excess reactant, to find how much product forms.
