Where fuels come from

Part 1 of 5

What is a fuel?

Fuel. A substance that releases useful energy when it reacts, usually by burning in oxygen.

A fuel stores chemical energy in its bonds. When it burns, that energy is released as heat and light.

Fuels fall into two groups, based on where they come from.

Part 2 of 5

Fossil fuels

Fossil fuels formed from the remains of plants and sea organisms that died long ago. Mud and rock buried them, and heat and pressure slowly changed them over millions of years.

FuelWhat it is
CoalA solid that is mostly carbon. It formed from buried plants.
Natural gasA gas that is mostly methane, CH4. It is found underground, often with oil.
PetrolA mixture of liquid hydrocarbons, such as octane, C8H18. It is separated from crude oil by fractional distillation.
Part 3 of 5

Renewable or not?

Renewable. A resource that natural processes can replace within a relatively short period of time.

We burn fossil fuels far faster than they form. So they are non-renewable.

A biofuel comes from a crop or waste that is replaced within a year or so. So biofuels are renewable.

Renewable does not mean the fuel gives off no carbon dioxide. It means the source can be replaced quickly.

Part 4 of 5

The three biofuels

BiofuelWhere it comes from
BiogasBacteria break down manure, food scraps or sewage without oxygen. The gas is mostly methane, CH4, with some carbon dioxide.
BioethanolYeast ferments glucose from crops such as sugarcane. Distillation then makes the ethanol purer.
BiodieselPlant oils, used cooking oil or animal fats are made into a fuel. You study how in Chapter 8.

Fermentation is the reaction that makes bioethanol.

C6H12O6(aq) → 2C2H5OH(aq) + 2CO2(g)
Check the balance. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms.
Part 5 of 5

Sunlight, glucose and fuel

Plants make glucose by photosynthesis. This converts light energy into chemical energy.

6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) → C6H12O6(aq) + 6O2(g)
Check the balance. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms.

Choose the fuel

Fuel choices depend on the place and the people. Pick a setting, choose a fuel, and give two reasons.

Worked example

Setting: A dairy farm with a lot of manure.

Choice: Biogas. Bacteria turn the manure into methane, so a waste becomes a fuel that the farm can use on site.

The cost is that the farm needs a sealed tank, and leaked methane is a strong greenhouse gas.

Key words

Fuel
A substance that releases useful energy when it reacts, usually by burning in oxygen.
Fossil fuel
A fuel made from the remains of organisms that died long ago. Coal, natural gas and petrol are fossil fuels.
Biofuel
A fuel made from recently living material such as crops, waste or animal fats.
Renewable
A resource that natural processes can replace within a relatively short period of time.
Non-renewable
A resource that is used much faster than natural processes can replace it.
Biogas
A gas, mostly methane, made when bacteria break down organic waste without oxygen.
Bioethanol
Ethanol made by fermenting glucose from crops, then distilling it.
Biodiesel
A fuel made from plant oils or animal fats.
Fermentation
A reaction in which yeast turns glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide.
Photosynthesis
The process plants use to turn light energy into chemical energy, making glucose and oxygen.
Glucose
A sugar with the formula C₆H₁₂O₆.
