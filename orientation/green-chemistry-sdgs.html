Which is greener?

Ibuprofen is one of the most used medicines in the world. Here are two real ways to make it. Both give the same pure ibuprofen.

Route A

  • Extra chemicalsAluminium chloride is used up in the first step.
  • WasteIt becomes a large amount of aluminium waste that must be treated.
  • HazardsUses several corrosive reagents, including aluminium chloride.

Route B

  • Extra chemicalsCatalysts speed up each step. They are recovered and reused.
  • WasteThe only by-product is acetic acid, which can be collected and sold.
  • HazardsUses hydrogen fluoride, a very toxic and corrosive acid. It is kept in a closed system and reused.
Decide

With a partner, choose the route you think is greener. Write one reason. There is no penalty for changing your mind later.

Judging sustainability

Part 1 of 7

Can the process keep going?

Sustainable means a process can carry on for a long time without running out of what it needs or causing harm that forces it to stop.

To judge a process, ask two questions. Can it be sustained? Is anything likely to limit it from continuing?

Limits can be land, water, energy or raw materials. For example, ethanol and biodiesel come from crops, and crops need land to grow.

That land could grow food instead. So there is an ethical question as well. Should the land grow food for fuel, or food for food?

Chemists use three tools to help answer these questions.

  1. Green chemistry principles judge the process itself (Parts 2 to 4).
  2. The Sustainable Development Goals judge the effect on people and the planet (Part 5).
  3. The circular economy judges what happens to materials after use (Part 6).
Part 2 of 7

Green chemistry and its seven principles

Green chemistry is the design of chemical products and processes that are safer and more sustainable than the ones used before.

Chemists Paul Anastas and John Warner wrote twelve principles of green chemistry. VCE Chemistry uses seven of them.

The main idea is to avoid making waste in the first place, so there is less to clean up afterwards.

PrincipleWhat it meansWhere you meet it
Atom economyAs many reactant atoms as possible end up in the product you want.8A Organic reactions

Part 3 of 7

What counts as waste?

Waste is any material a process makes that is not the product you want and that nobody uses.

A reaction that makes two or more products always makes a by-product. A by-product is waste only if it is thrown away.

For any substance that is not your product, ask one question. Is it collected and then used or sold?

If yes, it is a useful by-product and it is not waste. If no, it is waste.

In the ibuprofen routes, Route B’s acetic acid is collected and sold, so it is a useful by-product. Route A’s aluminium waste is treated and thrown away, so it is waste.

Carbon dioxide follows the same rule. CO2 released into the air is waste. CO2 that is captured and used, for example to carbonate drinks or to make urea, is a useful product.

Part 4 of 7

Atom economy and prevention of wastes

These two ideas are not the same. Atom economy is a calculation. Prevention of wastes is a judgement about the whole process.

Atom economy

It looks at: the by-products in the balanced equation.

It gives: a percentage.

It asks: how much of the reactant mass ends up in the product?

Prevention of wastes

It looks at: all the waste the whole process makes.

It gives: a judgement, made by weighing the evidence.

It asks: how much waste does the process make?

A reaction can have an atom economy of 100% and still make waste. Ethene and water make only ethanol, so there is no by-product. If the yield is below 100%, some ethene is left over, and that ethene is waste if it is thrown away.

Check yourself. A reaction has an atom economy of 100%. Can the process still make waste?

Yes. The process can still waste reactants left over from a yield below 100%, solvents, used-up catalysts or reagents, and waste from separating the product.

A first atom economy calculation

Atom economy = molar mass of the desired producttotal molar mass of all reactants × 100%
Ethanol from ethene and water: C2H4(g) + H2O(g) → C2H5OH(g)
Atom economy = 46.028.0 + 18.0 × 100% = 100%
Every reactant atom ends up in the ethanol. Addition reactions like this always have an atom economy of 100%.

Atom economy covers only the by-products in the equation. Prevention of wastes covers the whole process.

Key words

Green chemistry
The design of chemical products and processes that are safer and more sustainable than the ones used before.
Atom economy
The percentage of the reactants' mass that ends up in the product you want.
Catalysis
Using a catalyst, a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up.
Design for degradation
Making products that break down into harmless substances after use.
Design for energy efficiency
Making a process use as little energy as possible.
Designing safer chemicals
Making a product do its job with as little toxicity as possible.
Prevention of wastes
Avoiding waste in the first place, instead of treating it later.
Use of renewable feedstocks
Making products from raw materials that can be replaced quickly, mainly plants.
Feedstock
The raw material a process starts from.
Waste
Any material a process makes that is not the product you want and that nobody uses.
By-product
A substance made in a reaction that is not the product you want. It is waste only if nobody uses it.
Sustainable process
A process that can carry on for a long time without running out of what it needs or causing harm that forces it to stop.
Sustainable development
Meeting the needs of people today without stopping future generations from meeting theirs.
SDGs
The UN's 17 Sustainable Development Goals, to be reached by 2030.
Circular economy
A system where products and wastes are recovered and used again, instead of thrown away.
Watch out

"Green" does not just mean "uses plants". A process can use a renewable feedstock and still waste energy or make toxic by-products.

Watch out

A renewable fuel is not always sustainable. Growing the crop needs land, water and energy, and the land could grow food instead.

Watch out

A by-product is not always waste. If it is collected and used or sold, it is a useful by-product.

Watch out

An atom economy of 100% does not mean the process makes no waste. Leftover reactants, solvents and used-up catalysts count too.

Watch out

A catalyst is not a reactant. It is not used up, so it does not count in atom economy.
